Purpose and reach

This book is the law of the station. It applies to everyone aboard, from the Station Master to the newest crew member, in a department, a transit tube or the crawlways.

Its aim is to keep a small crew alive and working together far from home. Where a rule is unclear, read it the way that harms the fewest people. Nobody is above this book, and nobody is outside its protection.

Classes of offence

Offences come in three classes, and the class decides the sentence. An officer names the class when they charge someone.

ClassExamplesTypical sentenceNotes
Class 1, minor
  • Following someone through a door that asks for access you do not hold
  • Loitering in a work area after being asked to leave
  • Taking another department's tools without asking
  • Shouting over the radio
  • Leaving an open panel or a cut cable behind after a repair you started
A warning, spoken and written in the record. On a repeated offence, or an ignored warning, 1 to 3 minutes in a cellThe offender puts right what they spoiled.
Class 2, medium
  • Striking or threatening a crew member
  • Stealing locked equipment, such as medical supplies, EVA gear or an ID card
  • Cutting a cable, pulling a door's wires or switching off a room's power without a repair to justify it
  • Refusing a lawful order from an officer on duty
  • Using an ID card that is not yours
5 to 7 minutes in a cellWhat was stolen or misused goes back to its place. For damage, the offender repairs it, or works off its cost in duties.
Class 3, major
  • Anything that endangers the station or a whole department: opening the hull to space, wrecking the power plant, poisoning the air, locking people in a room without air
  • Causing a death by intent or gross carelessness
  • Taking a hostage
  • Breaking out of a cell, or helping someone to
10 to 15 minutes in a cell, then a hearing before the Station MasterThe Station Master may add a duty restriction, barring the offender from a department for the shift; the removal of their ID card's access; or removal from the shift, which puts them on the crew-transfer shuttle at its next departure.

Where one act fits two classes, the higher is charged. The same offence twice in one shift may be charged one class higher.

Contraband is hauling or hiding goods that carry a seal, or hindering an inspection, and it is charged as Class 2. Any officer may inspect a crate beside them, and the seal and the record are the evidence.

A paper written in another person's name, or a Request or a Report with a forged stamp, is forgery and is charged as Class 2. A paper cannot be told from a true one by its words: ask the person it names, and look at the stamp, the fax number and the fingerprints at the desk.

Sentences

A sentence is the least that keeps the crew safe, and it ends with the shift. Nothing here carries over to the next one.

Cell time runs in steps of 1 minute, 2 minutes, 3 minutes, 5 minutes, 7 minutes, 10 minutes, 12 minutes or 15 minutes.

A fine is a duty set in work. Only the Station Master or the Registrar may set one, and it never keeps anyone from eating or sleeping. Nobody who has served a sentence is charged again for the same act.

Rights of the detained

Whoever is held, in a cell or only stopped in a corridor, has these rights, and the officer must name them when asked.

  • To be told the offence and its class at the time they are stopped.
  • To be searched only for a stated reason, by an officer, with their things listed and returned on release.
  • To be free from harm. A detained person is not struck, starved or left without air. An officer who sees them hurt or ill calls a doctor at once and releases the cell if the doctor asks.
  • To be released the moment the sentence ends. The door counts the time itself: an officer may release early but never hold longer.
  • To ask for an Advocate, and to have the request passed on within a minute.
  • To have a mistake corrected: anyone wrongly held is released and the record is struck out.

Petition to an Advocate

An Advocate sits in the Advocate's Chambers. Their work is to see that the law is followed, not to take sides. Anyone detained, and anyone who saw it happen, may ask an Advocate to look at a case.

  1. Tell the nearest officer or a member of command, or go to the Advocate's Chambers window.
  2. Give your name, the person concerned, the charge and why you think it is wrong.
  3. Whoever hears it passes it to the Advocate's Chambers within one minute.
  4. The Advocate reads the officer's record and speaks to the detained person, the officer and any witnesses.
  5. They answer in one of three ways: the charge stands, it is lowered to a lesser class, or it is dropped.

An Advocate cannot open a cell door. They give the answer to the Watch Chief, or to the Station Master if there is none, who must act on it or give a written reason within five minutes. The sentence keeps running meanwhile, unless the Station Master orders a release.

The Advocate also weighs a seal that was found, a pledge that was signed freely and a petition made in good faith. They ask what was seen, by whom, and whether two kinds of evidence agree.

The Advocate may strike a Report that does not stand. The strike is written on the paper and in the record, and a charge that was forged is lost for good.

A petition made in good faith is never an offence.

Records

Every detention is written down: who was held, by whom, for what class, when and for how long. The Watch keeps the record, and any Advocate and the Station Master may read it. It states what was done, not what is thought of the person, and it is not used on a later shift.

If this book and the Station Master's order disagree, the order is followed now and the disagreement is written down for the Advocate's Chambers to settle.
