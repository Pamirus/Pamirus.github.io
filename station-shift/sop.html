Purpose

These procedures tell the Watch how to work and everyone else what to expect. They sit under Station Law: where the two differ, Station Law wins. The Watch protects the crew first, the station second and itself last.

Watch duties

  • Walk the corridors and tubes on a regular round and be seen: a visible officer prevents more trouble than a hidden one solves.
  • Answer calls for help at once. The nearest officer goes and says so on the radio, so that nobody is sent twice and nobody is missed.
  • Keep Holding clean, lit and supplied with air, keep the cell doors working and look in on whoever is inside.
  • Take complaints at the Watch desk by the public access area, and at the Watch Office. Write each one down, and never turn away someone with a problem, even if it is not the Watch's.
  • Help the other departments: stand by a doctor treating someone dangerous, escort valuable cargo, and open a door for a crew member who has lost an ID card once you have checked who they are.

Alert levels

The station is always on one of three levels. The Station Master sets the level. The Watch Chief may raise it from Calm to Swell if the Station Master cannot be reached. Only the Station Master lowers it.

CalmNormal duty.Officers patrol singly and doors work as usual. A search needs a clear reason and the person's agreement or the Station Master's order.
SwellA danger is suspected but not confirmed, such as a threat, a stolen restricted item or a hull alarm with no cause found.Officers patrol in pairs with their equipment in view and may ask anyone in a corridor to show an ID card. GaleA danger is confirmed and present, such as a breach, a power failure in a department, a hostage or a violent death.All officers report to the Watch Office and are assigned by the Watch Chief, and Holding is made ready. Officers may search anyone in the danger area without asking, though every rule about treating people properly stays. Crew not needed there keep clear.

When the danger has passed, the Station Master lowers the level one step at a time, with a word on the radio at each step.

Searching and detaining

  1. Before you search or stop anyone, know your reason and say it in one sentence. If you cannot, do not do it. Ask first, speak plainly and keep your distance. Use the least force that works and stop the moment it is no longer needed.
  2. Search in the open, with a second officer or a witness near. Take only what the rule allows, write down what you took and give it back on release, unless it is evidence of a Class 2 or Class 3 offence, when it goes to the Watch Chief.
  3. Detain only for an offence in Station Law, and name its class aloud. Escort the person to Holding yourself by the shortest safe way, stopping nowhere but at a doctor. If they are hurt, ill or short of air, call a doctor before anything else.

Belt and force

A Watch belt holds a taser, a baton, a pepper spray, handcuffs and a flashlight, and no radio.

Use the least force that works, in this order: speak, then pepper spray within two tiles, then the taser for a danger to others, and the baton only against an attack within reach. Never use any of them on someone cuffed, down or dead.

Once the person stops, cuff them, call a doctor if they are hurt, and write down which items you used and why.

Use of the cells

  1. At a cell door's panel, an officer or the Station Master picks the time from 1 minute, 2 minutes, 3 minutes, 5 minutes, 7 minutes, 10 minutes, 12 minutes or 15 minutes; the door counts it and shows the time left. Match the class: 1 to 3 minutes for a repeated Class 1 offence, 5 or 7 for Class 2, 10 to 15 for Class 3.
  2. Take belongings first: what the prisoner carries, not what they wear, goes into the cell's property locker, which only the Watch opens, and returns on release if they are still in the cell or Holding. The cell doors and the way out by the hallway open from inside for anyone, so a released prisoner walks out alone, never into the tunnels.
  3. Lock the door only with the person inside, one person to a cell unless the Watch Chief says otherwise, and never someone who needs a doctor. While it is locked nobody opens it from either side, with a card or a tool. An officer or the Station Master may release it early at the door, and should if the person falls ill, if an Advocate's answer says so, or if the cell loses air.
  4. A cell holds people who have been charged. It is not a store, a hiding place or a way of keeping someone past the sentence. Write the time, the sentence, the person's name and your own in the record.

Reporting

  1. Report by radio as soon as it is safe: what is happening, what you are doing, where you are. Keep it short.
  2. Afterwards write the full report: who, what, where and when, then what was done and what was found. Leave out guesses, or mark them as guesses.
  3. Hand the report to the Watch Chief, or to the Station Master if there is none, and a copy of any detention to the Advocate's Chambers. If you made a mistake, say so in it: a mistake that is reported can be mended.

A complaint against an officer is taken as seriously as any other and goes at once to the Watch Chief and the Advocate's Chambers.
